Introduction to Endoscopy

Understanding the fundamentals of gastrointestinal endoscopy and its revolutionary impact on modern medicine.

What is Endoscopy?

A minimally invasive procedure using a flexible tube with a camera to examine the digestive tract, enabling direct visualization of mucosal surfaces.

Purpose & Importance

Essential for diagnosis, biopsy, and treatment of GI diseases. Gold standard for detecting cancers, ulcers, inflammation, and bleeding sources.

Brief History

From rigid scopes in 1868 to modern flexible fiber-optic and video endoscopes. Revolutionary advances in the 1950s transformed GI medicine forever.

Upper GI Endoscopy (EGD)

Esophagogastroduodenoscopy examines esophagus, stomach, and duodenum.

Purpose: Diagnose GERD, ulcers, celiac disease, H. pylori infection
Instruments: Flexible endoscope (8-10mm), biopsy forceps, brush cytology
Preparation: NPO 6-8 hours, remove dentures, consent form

Risks

  • • Perforation (0.03%)
  • • Bleeding (0.01%)
  • • Aspiration pneumonia
  • • Cardiopulmonary events

Indications

  • • Dysphagia/odynophagia
  • • Upper GI bleeding
  • • Abdominal pain
  • • Abnormal imaging
  • • Surveillance Barrett's
Lower GI

Colonoscopy

Complete examination of the entire colon and terminal ileum using a flexible colonoscope.

Purpose: Screen for colorectal cancer, remove polyps, evaluate IBD
Instruments: Colonoscope (160cm), polypectomy snare, biopsy forceps
Preparation: Bowel prep (PEG solution), clear liquid diet 24h prior

Risks

  • • Perforation (0.1-0.5%)
  • • Post-polypectomy bleeding
  • • Cardiopulmonary complications
  • • Infection (rare)

Indications

  • • CRC screening (age 45+)
  • • Positive FIT/FOBT
  • • Change in bowel habits
  • • Unexplained weight loss
  • • IBD surveillance

Examines rectum, sigmoid, and descending colon (60cm scope). Often used without sedation.

Capsule Endoscopy

Common Diseases Diagnosed

GERD

Gastroesophageal reflux disease with esophagitis, Barrett's metaplasia.

Endoscopic findings: Erythema, erosions, strictures, Barrett's esophagus (salmon-colored mucosa).

Management: PPI therapy, lifestyle modifications, surveillance for Barrett's.

Peptic Ulcer

Gastric and duodenal ulcers from H. pylori or NSAID use.

Endoscopic findings: Round/oval mucosal breaks with white base, surrounding erythema.

Forrest Classification: Ia (spurting), Ib (oozing), IIa (visible vessel), IIb (adherent clot), IIc (flat pigmented spot), III (clean base).

Gastritis

Inflammation of gastric mucosa, acute or chronic.

Endoscopic findings: Erythematous, edematous mucosa, erosions, exudates, atrophy in chronic cases.

Sydney System: Classification based on site, morphology, etiology (H. pylori, autoimmune, chemical).

Esophageal Varices

Dilated submucosal veins from portal hypertension.

Endoscopic findings: Tortuous veins protruding into lumen, red wale signs, cherry red spots.

Grading: Small (straight), Medium (tortuous <1/3 lumen), Large (>1/3 lumen). Band ligation for treatment.

Diseases Detected

Colorectal Cancer

Third most common cancer worldwide. Endoscopy enables early detection and curative resection of precancerous polyps.

Polyps

Adenomatous polyps are precancerous. Types: tubular, villous, tubulovillous. Size >1cm, high-grade dysplasia, villous features increase cancer risk.

Ulcerative Colitis

Continuous mucosal inflammation from rectum proximally. Endoscopy shows erythema, granularity, friability, ulcerations, loss of vascular pattern.

Crohn's Disease

Transmural, skip lesions. Cobblestoning, deep ulcers, strictures, fistulas. Can affect any part of GI tract from mouth to anus.

Section 06

Risks & Complications

Understanding potential complications ensures better patient safety and informed consent.

Bleeding

Most common complication, especially post-polypectomy or biopsy.

Incidence: 0.01-1.5%
Management: Epinephrine injection, clips, thermal coagulation

Perforation

Full-thickness tear of GI wall. Surgical emergency if large.

Incidence: 0.03-0.5%
Management: Surgical repair, endoscopic clipping, antibiotics

Infection

Rare due to rigorous disinfection protocols. Risk with ERCP.

Incidence: <0.1%
Prevention: High-level disinfection, prophylactic antibiotics for high-risk patients

Sedation Reactions

Respiratory depression, hypotension, allergic reactions to anesthetic agents.

Prevention: ASA screening, continuous monitoring, reversal agents available
Management: Flumazenil, naloxone, airway support

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions patients and students ask about endoscopy.

No, endoscopy is not painful due to sedation and anesthesia. Patients receive conscious sedation (midazolam, fentanyl, or propofol) making them relaxed and drowsy. A topical anesthetic spray numbs the throat for upper endoscopy. Some pressure or bloating may be felt during colonoscopy as air is insufflated, but significant pain is uncommon. Patient comfort is continuously monitored by the nursing team.

Upper endoscopy (EGD) typically takes 5-15 minutes. Colonoscopy usually requires 20-45 minutes depending on anatomy, preparation quality, and whether interventions like polypectomy are performed. ERCP procedures may take 30-90 minutes. Total facility time including preparation and recovery is 2-4 hours. Patients should plan to rest for the remainder of the day after sedation.

Sedation is standard for colonoscopy and ERCP due to procedure duration and discomfort. For EGD, sedation is commonly used but unsedated procedures are possible in select patients. Flexible sigmoidoscopy is often performed without sedation. The choice depends on patient preference, anxiety level, comorbidities, and procedure complexity. Unsedated procedures allow faster recovery and no escort requirement.

Serious complications are rare (<0.5%). Major risks include perforation (tear in GI wall), significant bleeding (especially after polypectomy), infection, and adverse reactions to sedation. Cardiopulmonary events are the most common serious complications in elderly or cardiac patients. Risk factors include advanced age, comorbidities, therapeutic procedures, and anatomical challenges. Emergency protocols and surgical backup are always available.

For EGD: Nothing to eat or drink for 6-8 hours. Remove dentures, glasses, and jewelry. Arrange transportation if sedated. For colonoscopy: Follow a clear liquid diet for 24 hours before. Complete the prescribed bowel preparation (usually polyethylene glycol solution) the day before and morning of procedure. A clean colon is essential for accurate examination. Poor preparation may require rescheduling.

The Importance of GI Endoscopy

Gastrointestinal endoscopy remains one of the most transformative tools in modern medicine. From detecting early-stage cancers to managing acute bleeding, these procedures save countless lives daily. For nursing and medical students, mastering endoscopy concepts is essential for providing exceptional patient care.

Early screening, particularly colonoscopy at age 45, can prevent up to 90% of colorectal cancers through polyp removal. Patient education, thorough preparation, and vigilant nursing care ensure optimal outcomes and patient safety.
