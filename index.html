Module 01

Introduction to Endoscopy

Understanding the fundamentals of gastrointestinal endoscopy.

What is Endoscopy?

Endoscopy is a minimally invasive diagnostic and therapeutic procedure that involves inserting a long, thin, flexible tube with a camera attached (endoscope) into the body to visualize internal organs and structures. In gastroenterology, it allows direct visualization of the mucosal lining of the digestive tract.

Visualization

Direct mucosal examination with high-definition imaging

Intervention

Biopsy, polypectomy, hemostasis, dilation

Safety

Low complication rate with high diagnostic yield

The Endoscope

  • 1 Insertion tube: Flexible, 8-13mm diameter, contains channels
  • 2 Light source: LED or fiber-optic illumination
  • 3 Camera: CCD or CMOS chip at distal tip
  • 4 Instrument channel: For biopsy, irrigation, suction
  • 5 Control section: Angulation knobs, buttons, ports

Working Channels

Air/Water Channel
Insufflation and lens cleaning
Biopsy Channel
2.8-3.7mm for instruments
Suction Channel
Fluid and debris removal

Upper GI Endoscopy

EGD procedure, anatomy, and disease diagnosis.

Upper GI Tract

Hover over an organ to see details

Procedure Timeline

1

Pre-procedure Assessment

Verify NPO status, review allergies, check vitals, establish IV access, obtain informed consent.

2

Sedation & Monitoring

Midazolam + Fentanyl or Propofol. Continuous SpO2, BP, HR, ECG monitoring by nursing staff.

3

Scope Insertion

Topical oropharyngeal anesthesia, mouthguard placed, scope passed under direct vision: esophagus → stomach → duodenum.

4

Examination & Biopsy

Systematic inspection, photo documentation, tissue sampling, H. pylori testing, therapeutic interventions if indicated.

5

Recovery

Monitor until awake, check gag reflex before oral intake, document findings, discharge instructions.

Common Diseases Diagnosed

Endoscopic findings: Erythema, erosions, strictures, Barrett's esophagus (salmon-colored mucosa replacing normal pale pink).

Grades (Los Angeles): A (one mucosal break ≤5mm) to D (circumferential mucosal breaks).

Management: PPI therapy, lifestyle modifications, surveillance endoscopy for Barrett's every 3-5 years.

Endoscopic findings: Round/oval mucosal breaks with white fibrin base, surrounding erythema. Gastric ulcers typically on lesser curvature; duodenal in bulb.

Forrest Classification for bleeding ulcers: Ia (spurting), Ib (oozing), IIa (visible vessel), IIb (adherent clot), IIc (flat pigmented spot), III (clean base).

Management: Test for H. pylori, PPI therapy, endoscopic hemostasis for active bleeding.

Endoscopic findings: Erythematous, edematous mucosa, erosions, exudates, friability. Atrophy and intestinal metaplasia in chronic cases.

Sydney System: Classification by site (antrum/body), morphology (erosive/non-erosive), and etiology (H. pylori, autoimmune, chemical/NSAID).

Endoscopic findings: Tortuous veins protruding into lumen. Red wale signs (longitudinal red streaks), cherry red spots indicate high bleeding risk.

Grading: Small (straight, minimally elevated), Medium (tortuous, <1/3 lumen), Large (>1/3 lumen).

Management: Non-selective beta-blockers, endoscopic variceal ligation (EVL) for primary prophylaxis and acute bleeding.

Diseases Detected

Colorectal Cancer

Third most common cancer. Endoscopy enables early detection and curative polypectomy. 5-year survival >90% when caught early.

Polyps

Adenomatous polyps are precancerous. Types: tubular, villous, tubulovillous. Size >1cm and villous features increase cancer risk.

Ulcerative Colitis

Continuous inflammation from rectum proximally. Erythema, granularity, friability, ulcerations, loss of vascular pattern.

Crohn's Disease

Transmural, skip lesions. Cobblestoning, deep ulcers, strictures, fistulas. Can affect any GI segment from mouth to anus.

Module 05

Patient Preparation

Before, during, and after the procedure.

Pre-Procedure Checklist

Nursing Note

Always verify the correct procedure and site with the patient using two identifiers. Check for dentures, eyeglasses, and hearing aids before EGD.

Intra-Procedure Monitoring

Vital Signs

BP, HR, respiratory rate every 5-15 minutes or continuous automated monitoring.

Oxygen Saturation

Continuous pulse oximetry. Supplemental O2 at 2-4L/min via nasal cannula standard.

ECG Monitoring

Continuous cardiac monitoring for patients with cardiac history or advanced age.

Airway Management

Suction available, jaw thrust if needed, BVM ready for respiratory depression.

Post-Procedure Recovery

1

Recovery Room Monitoring

Monitor using Modified Aldrete Score (activity, respiration, circulation, consciousness, SpO2). Score ≥9 for discharge.

2

Diet Resumption

EGD: NPO until gag reflex returns (usually 1 hour). Colonoscopy: clear liquids, advance as tolerated. No alcohol for 24h.

3

Discharge Education

Warn about: severe abdominal pain, fever >100.4°F, heavy bleeding, dizziness. No driving or machinery for 24 hours post-sedation.

Colonoscopy Bowel Prep Timeline

1
7 Days Before

Stop iron supplements, aspirin (if advised), anticoagulants per physician. Arrange transportation.

2
Day Before - Morning

Begin clear liquid diet only: water, clear broths, plain coffee/tea (no milk), clear apple/white grape juice, Jello (no red/purple), popsicles.

3
Day Before - Evening

Start bowel prep solution (PEG-3350, e.g., GoLYTELY, MiraLAX). Drink 8oz every 10-15 minutes until 2L consumed. Stay near bathroom.

4
Procedure Day

Complete remaining prep (split-dose protocol). Nothing by mouth 2-4 hours before procedure. Stools should be clear/yellow liquid.

Module 06

Risks & Complications

Understanding complications for patient safety and informed consent.

Bleeding

Most common complication. Risk increases with polypectomy, biopsy of vascular lesions, or coagulopathy.

Incidence 0.01-1.5%
Risk Factors Anticoagulants, large polyps, hot biopsy
Management: Epinephrine injection, hemoclips, thermal coagulation, balloon tamponade for varices.

Perforation

Full-thickness tear of GI wall. Surgical emergency if large or contaminated.

Incidence 0.03-0.5%
Risk Factors Therapeutic procedures, stricture dilation, diverticula
Management: Endoscopic clipping (small), surgical repair (large), IV antibiotics, NPO.

Infection

Rare due to rigorous high-level disinfection protocols. Higher risk with ERCP and immunocompromised patients.

Incidence <0.1%
Prevention: High-level disinfection (glutaraldehyde, peracetic acid), single-use accessories, prophylactic antibiotics for high-risk ERCP.

Sedation Reactions

Respiratory depression, hypotension, paradoxical agitation, or allergic reactions to anesthetic agents.

Incidence 0.1-2%
Management: Flumazenil (benzodiazepine reversal), naloxone (opioid reversal), airway support, supplemental O2, BVM if needed.

Risk Stratification (ASA Classification)

ASA I-II

Healthy patient or mild systemic disease. Standard sedation protocols apply.

ASA III

Severe systemic disease. Modified sedation, possible anesthesia consult.

ASA IV-V

Life-threatening systemic disease. Anesthesia-managed sedation strongly recommended.

Module 10

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions from patients and students.

No, endoscopy is not painful due to sedation and anesthesia. Patients receive conscious sedation (midazolam, fentanyl, or propofol) making them relaxed and drowsy. A topical anesthetic spray numbs the throat for upper endoscopy. Some pressure or bloating may be felt during colonoscopy as air is insufflated, but significant pain is uncommon. Patient comfort is continuously monitored by the nursing team.

Upper endoscopy (EGD) typically takes 5-15 minutes. Colonoscopy usually requires 20-45 minutes depending on anatomy, preparation quality, and whether interventions like polypectomy are performed. ERCP procedures may take 30-90 minutes. Total facility time including preparation and recovery is 2-4 hours. Patients should plan to rest for the remainder of the day after sedation.

Sedation is standard for colonoscopy and ERCP due to procedure duration and discomfort. For EGD, sedation is commonly used but unsedated procedures are possible in select patients. Flexible sigmoidoscopy is often performed without sedation. The choice depends on patient preference, anxiety level, comorbidities, and procedure complexity. Unsedated procedures allow faster recovery and no escort requirement.

Serious complications are rare (<0.5%). Major risks include perforation (tear in GI wall), significant bleeding (especially after polypectomy), infection, and adverse reactions to sedation. Cardiopulmonary events are the most common serious complications in elderly or cardiac patients. Risk factors include advanced age, comorbidities, therapeutic procedures, and anatomical challenges. Emergency protocols and surgical backup are always available.

For EGD: Nothing to eat or drink for 6-8 hours. Remove dentures, glasses, and jewelry. Arrange transportation if sedated. For colonoscopy: Follow a clear liquid diet for 24 hours before. Complete the prescribed bowel preparation (usually polyethylene glycol solution) the day before and morning of procedure. A clean colon is essential for accurate examination. Poor preparation may require rescheduling.
